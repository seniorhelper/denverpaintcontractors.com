Why exterior paint fails in Colorado: peeling, chalking and fading explained (and how to stop it)

Altitude sun, freeze-thaw and sprinkler overspray are hard on paint. Here's how to read what your house is telling you, and what actually fixes it.

By Bighorn PaintingUpdated September 16, 202615 minute read

Key takeaways

  • UV increases roughly 10 percent per 1,000 meters of altitude, per the WHO, so Denver-area walls take more sun than homes at sea level.
  • Peeling usually points to moisture or poor adhesion. Chalking and fading point to UV breaking down the paint film.
  • Freeze-thaw cycles turn small caulk gaps and cracks into peeling paint fast.
  • Sprinklers hitting siding are a common, overlooked cause of peeling on metro homes.
  • Most failures are prevented by prep: washing, scraping, sanding, caulking, spot-priming and the right product for the surface.

The quick answer

Exterior paint in Colorado fails for the same basic reasons it fails anywhere, moisture, sun and poor adhesion, but the Front Range turns up the intensity. Stronger UV at altitude breaks paint down faster on sun-facing walls. Constant freeze-thaw cycles pry paint loose wherever water gets behind it. Dry air and wind make paint dry too fast during application. And big daily temperature swings keep siding and trim moving.

The good news: almost every one of these failures is predictable, and most are preventable. Knowing what you're looking at helps you decide whether you need a touch-up, targeted repairs or a full repaint, and it helps you spot a contractor who's about to paint right over the problem.

What makes Colorado so hard on house paint

More UV at altitude

The World Health Organization explains that there's less atmosphere to absorb UV at higher elevations, and UV levels rise by about 10 percent for every 1,000 meters of altitude. Denver sits around 1,600 meters, and many suburbs and foothills communities sit higher. UV attacks the binders in paint, the ingredients that hold pigment together and stick it to your house. As binders break down, paint loses color, turns powdery and becomes more brittle.

Freeze-thaw cycles

Along the Front Range, winter and shoulder-season temperatures swing above and below freezing again and again. When water gets into a crack, an open joint or behind loose paint, it freezes and expands overnight, then thaws during the day. Repeat that dozens of times, and small defects grow into peeling sections. The National Weather Service records show Denver freezes can arrive as early as September and linger as late as June, so the stress season is long.

Dry air and wind

Low humidity helps with some things, like fewer mildew problems than humid climates. But during painting, dry air, wind and sun can make paint skin over before it levels and bonds properly. That can leave lap marks and weaker films if the work isn't timed well.

Big daily temperature swings

Wood, fiber cement, vinyl and metal all expand and contract with temperature. A wall that's cold at sunrise and hot by mid-afternoon moves every day. Rigid, brittle paint and cheap caulk crack under that movement.

Hail

Hailstorms chip trim and siding, dent garage doors, and can crack older paint that's already brittle from sun exposure. Once the surface is broken, moisture gets in.

Sprinklers

This one surprises people. Many metro homes have irrigation heads that spray siding, trim and fences every morning all summer. Constantly wetting the bottom rows of siding keeps wood damp, pushes water into joints and is a common reason paint peels along the lower few feet of a house.

Peeling and flaking paint

Peeling is the failure most Denver homeowners notice first, usually on trim, window sills, fascia boards and the bottom of siding. It means the paint has lost its grip on the surface underneath.

Why it happens

  • Moisture getting behind the paint. Through open caulk joints, cracks, gutters overflowing, sprinkler overspray, or moisture moving from inside the home.
  • Painting over a bad surface. Dirt, chalk and loose old paint give new paint nothing solid to bond to.
  • Skipped primer on bare wood. Raw wood that's been weathering needs primer so topcoats adhere.
  • Freeze-thaw. Water trapped under paint freezes and pushes it off.

How it's fixed

First, find and fix the water source. Repainting over a sprinkler problem or a leaking gutter just resets the clock. Then scrape loose paint back to a firm edge, sand the transitions so they don't show, spot-prime bare areas and apply finish coats. On heavily peeled surfaces, more extensive removal may be needed.

If your home was built before 1978, peeling paint may contain lead. The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule requires certified firms and lead-safe work practices when painted surfaces in those homes are disturbed. Don't dry-scrape or sand old paint yourself without understanding the risks.

Chalking: the powder test

Rub your palm across a sunny wall. If it comes away with a powdery film in the color of your house, the paint is chalking.

Why it happens

Some chalking is normal as exterior paint ages. UV breaks down the binder at the surface, freeing pigment particles that rub off. Lower-quality paints and high UV exposure speed it up, which is why chalking is common on south and west walls in Colorado.

Why it matters

Heavy chalk isn't just ugly. New paint applied over chalk can't bond well, so it's a setup for peeling. That's why washing is a core prep step, and why heavily chalked surfaces may need a primer designed to bind remaining chalk.

How it's fixed

Thorough washing, letting the surface dry, priming where chalk remains, and repainting with a quality exterior line suited to the exposure.

Fading and uneven color

If the side of your house that faces the street looks lighter than the side yard, or a wall that bakes all afternoon looks washed out next to a shaded one, that's UV fading.

What affects fading

  • Exposure. South and west walls fade first.
  • Color and pigments. Some bright and deep colors are more prone to fading than others. Paint makers note UV resistance for certain colors.
  • Product quality. Better exterior lines are generally formulated for improved color retention.

Fading on its own is cosmetic, but it's often the early sign that the paint film is aging, and chalking and eventually cracking tend to follow. Repainting at the fading stage is usually cheaper than waiting until prep work multiplies.

Choosing colors with Colorado's light and exposure in mind helps. Our color selection guide covers how colors read at altitude.

Cracking, checking and alligatoring

Hairline cracks, a pattern like dried mud, or scaly "alligator skin" usually mean the paint has become brittle and can no longer flex with the surface.

Why it happens

  • Many layers of old paint building up over decades
  • Coatings that aren't compatible with each other, such as some older oil-based layers under newer finishes
  • Paint applied too thick in one coat
  • Long-term UV exposure and constant temperature swings

How it's fixed

Because the problem lives in the old layers, recoating on top doesn't solve it. The failing paint needs to be removed or sanded back to a sound, smooth surface, then primed and repainted. This is prep-heavy, which is why older Denver homes often land toward the higher end of painting estimates. Our Denver painting cost guide explains how prep affects price.

Blistering and bubbling

Blisters are bubbles under the paint film. Pop one and check what's underneath.

  • Bare wood or old paint under the blister usually points to moisture pushing out from behind.
  • Another paint layer under the blister can point to heat, such as painting a wall in hot direct sun, or painting over a damp surface.

The fix depends on the cause: find the moisture source if there is one, scrape and let the surface dry, prime and repaint in proper conditions. Timing matters, and our guide to the best time to paint an exterior in Colorado covers how crews avoid heat-related blistering.

Other problems Front Range homes see

Mildew

Colorado's dry climate means less mildew than humid regions, but it still shows up on shaded north walls, under decks, and wherever sprinklers keep siding wet. Dark spots that don't rinse off easily may be mildew. It needs proper cleaning before painting, or new paint can fail over it.

Tannin bleed on cedar

Cedar and redwood contain natural tannins that can bleed through light-colored paint as brown or orange stains. A stain-blocking primer made for tannin-rich woods prevents it.

Efflorescence on masonry

A white, crusty residue on brick or stucco is often efflorescence, salts left behind as water moves through masonry. It signals a moisture path. Painting over it without addressing the water tends to fail. Masonry also needs coatings that let it breathe.

Caulk failure

Caulk is the unsung hero of exterior paint. When it shrinks, cracks or pulls away, water gets in. In Colorado's temperature swings, cheap caulk fails fast. Exterior-rated, flexible caulk in properly prepared joints makes a big difference.

Rust

Rust spots or streaks usually come from corroding nail heads or metal trim. They need to be treated and spot-primed with a rust-inhibiting primer, or the stains come right back.

Vinyl siding warping

Painting vinyl siding a darker color than it was designed for can make it absorb more heat and warp. Sherwin-Williams VinylSafe and PPG SidingSafe color collections are designed for painting vinyl.

A 10-minute exterior inspection checklist

Do this walk-around each spring after winter and each fall before it. Take photos as you go.

  1. Start on the sunniest side. Look for fading and do the chalk test with your palm.
  2. Check trim and window sills. These fail first. Look for peeling, cracking and soft wood.
  3. Look at caulk joints. Around windows, doors, corner boards and where trim meets siding. Any gaps?
  4. Check the bottom rows of siding. Especially where sprinklers hit. Peeling, swelling or stains?
  5. Look up at fascia and soffits. Peeling here often points to gutter overflow or ice dams.
  6. Inspect garage doors and front doors. Hail chips and sun fading show up here.
  7. Press on suspect wood. Soft or spongy spots may mean rot that needs repair before paint.
  8. Check masonry. Cracks in stucco, white crust on brick or peeling paint on masonry.
  9. Look at the deck and fence. Graying, splitting and water soaking in instead of beading.
  10. Check your sprinklers. Run them briefly and see what they hit.

If you check two or three of these, it's worth getting a professional opinion before the next freeze-thaw season makes it worse.

How to prevent exterior paint failure in Colorado

1. Prep is most of the job

Washing, scraping loose paint, sanding transitions, caulking open joints and spot-priming bare wood aren't optional extras. They're what keep paint on the house. When comparing quotes, look for these steps in writing.

2. Choose products for the surface and exposure

Quality exterior acrylic paints are commonly used on Colorado siding and trim because they stay flexible and hold up to sun. Masonry needs masonry-appropriate coatings. Vinyl needs vinyl-safe colors. Tannin-rich wood needs a stain-blocking primer. Bighorn primarily uses Sherwin-Williams and PPG and names the product line for each surface on the estimate.

3. Apply in the right conditions

Follow label temperatures, watch overnight lows, keep paint out of the harshest direct sun, avoid damp surfaces and plan around storms.

4. Fix water problems first

Adjust sprinklers so they don't hit siding. Clean and repair gutters. Make sure downspouts carry water away. No paint wins a fight with a constant water source.

5. Maintain between repaints

  • Do the spring and fall walk-around above
  • Gently wash dirty areas
  • Recaulk small gaps before they grow
  • Touch up small chips and scrapes before water gets in
  • Keep decks and fences sealed on schedule

Where a workmanship warranty fits in

A workmanship warranty is a contractor's promise about how the job was done: the prep and the application. It's different from the paint maker's product warranty. Because so many Colorado paint failures trace back to prep and application, a written workmanship warranty from a company you can still reach years later is worth a lot. Surface by surface: what fails and what works

Wood lap siding and wood trim

Wood is beautiful and forgiving to paint, but it moves with moisture and temperature, and bare wood weathers quickly in Colorado sun. It's the most prep-sensitive exterior surface. Common failures are peeling at end grain and window sills, cracking on old layers, and rot where water sits. What works: thorough scraping and sanding, priming bare wood, sealing joints and end grain, and keeping sprinklers and snow off the lower boards.

Fiber cement siding

Fiber cement holds paint well when it's clean and the joints are sealed properly. Failures usually trace back to open butt joints, damaged factory edges or painting over dirt and chalk. What works: careful washing, flexible exterior caulk where the manufacturer allows it, and a quality exterior acrylic.

Hardboard or masonite-style siding

Common on many 1980s and 1990s metro homes, hardboard siding performs well as long as it stays sealed. Once water gets into cut edges or a damaged face, boards can swell and soften. What works: inspecting for damage, replacing boards that are swollen or soft, sealing and priming edges, and keeping irrigation off the bottom courses.

Stucco

Stucco is durable, but hairline and settlement cracks let water in, and painting over efflorescence or loose material fails. What works: addressing cracks appropriately, cleaning thoroughly, and using a coating made for masonry that allows the wall to breathe.

Brick

Unpainted brick can last a very long time on its own. Once painted, it's hard to go back, and the wrong coating can trap moisture. What works: a careful decision about whether to paint at all, then cleaning, dealing with any efflorescence and moisture issues, and using a breathable masonry product.

Vinyl and aluminum siding

Vinyl can be painted, but color choice matters because darker colors absorb heat and can warp the panels. Aluminum siding often chalks heavily with age. What works: thorough cleaning, choosing from vinyl-safe color collections like Sherwin-Williams VinylSafe or PPG SidingSafe, and products rated for the substrate.

Metal railings and doors

Metal fails through rust. What works: removing loose rust and scale, priming with a rust-inhibiting primer, and applying a finish suited to metal.

Five exterior paint myths that cost Colorado homeowners money

Myth 1: "Paint and primer in one means you never need primer."

Self-priming paints can work well over sound, previously painted surfaces. Bare wood, stains, heavy chalk, tannin-rich cedar and some slick or problem surfaces still typically benefit from a dedicated primer. The surface decides, not the label on the front of the can.

Myth 2: "A fresh coat will fix peeling."

New paint over peeling paint only adds weight to a film that's already letting go. Until loose paint is removed and the cause is fixed, the new coat is on borrowed time.

Myth 3: "Colorado is dry, so moisture isn't a problem."

Low humidity doesn't stop snowmelt, sprinklers, gutter overflow, ice dams or freeze-thaw. Moisture is still the number one reason paint lets go on Front Range homes.

Myth 4: "More paint is always better."

Coverage matters, but paint applied far thicker than the manufacturer recommends can cure poorly and crack. The right number of properly applied coats beats one heavy coat.

Myth 5: "All exterior paints are basically the same."

Paint makers sell a wide range of exterior lines at very different quality levels, and they differ in how well they hold color, resist dirt and stay flexible. In high-UV, high-swing climates, the gap between a bargain paint and a quality exterior line shows up faster.

The bottom line

Colorado's altitude sun, freeze-thaw cycles, dry air, temperature swings, hail and even sprinklers all conspire against exterior paint. Peeling usually means moisture or poor adhesion. Chalking and fading mean UV is breaking the paint down. Cracking means old layers have gone brittle. The fix is almost never "just paint over it." It's finding the cause, doing the prep, choosing products for each surface, and applying them in the right conditions.

Frequently asked questions

Why is the paint peeling on my house in Denver?

Peeling usually means moisture is getting behind the paint or the paint never bonded well. Common causes include open caulk joints, sprinklers hitting siding, gutter overflow, painting over dirt or chalk, and skipped primer on bare wood. Freeze-thaw cycles make peeling spread faster in Colorado.

What causes exterior paint to chalk?

Chalking happens when UV breaks down the paint binder at the surface, leaving a powdery residue. It is more common with lower-quality paints and on sun-facing walls, and UV levels are higher at Colorado's elevation.

Does altitude affect house paint?

Yes. The World Health Organization notes UV levels rise about 10 percent for every 1,000 meters of altitude. More UV speeds up fading and chalking, which is why product choice matters on sun-facing walls in the Denver area.

Can you paint over peeling paint?

Not successfully. Loose paint needs to be scraped back to a sound edge, transitions sanded, bare areas primed and the moisture cause fixed first. Painting over peeling paint traps the problem and the new paint fails with it.

Why does paint peel at the bottom of my siding?

Peeling along the lower rows of siding is often caused by sprinklers spraying the house, splash-back, or snow sitting against the wall. Adjusting irrigation and fixing drainage should come before repainting.

How often should a house be painted in Colorado?

There is no single number. It depends on exposure, siding type, product quality and how well the previous job was prepped. A spring and fall inspection for fading, chalking, peeling and open caulk is the best guide.

What is the white residue on my brick or stucco?

A white, crusty residue on masonry is often efflorescence, salts left behind as water moves through the material. It points to a moisture source that should be addressed before painting.

Is peeling paint on an older home dangerous?

Homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint. The EPA's Renovation, Repair and Painting rule requires certified firms and lead-safe work practices when disturbing those painted surfaces, so avoid dry-scraping or sanding old paint without the right precautions.
