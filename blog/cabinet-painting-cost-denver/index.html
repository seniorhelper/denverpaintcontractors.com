The short answer

In the Denver metro, professional kitchen cabinet refinishing commonly lands between $2,000 and $8,000, with most full-kitchen projects falling in the middle of that range. Published Denver cost data from multiple sources clusters in the same place: one local cost guide puts typical full-kitchen refinishing at $2,000 to $6,500, another at $3,000 to $8,000, and national aggregators show averages inside those bounds.

Replacement is a different financial category. Denver replacement quotes commonly start around $15,000 and climb past $30,000 once cabinetry, demolition, installation and the countertop work that comes with it are included.

That gap is why cabinet painting is one of the highest-return projects available to a homeowner who likes their layout. You are changing everything you see for a fraction of what it costs to change everything you have.

How cabinet pricing is actually built

Most homeowners think in square feet. Cabinet professionals think in pieces, because pieces are what get handled, sanded, primed, sprayed, dried, flipped and reinstalled.

The unit of measure is the door

A kitchen with 30 doors and drawer fronts takes roughly twice the labor of a kitchen with 15, even if the rooms are the same size. Each piece gets removed, labeled, cleaned, sanded, primed, sprayed on both sides with cure time between coats, and reinstalled with its hardware.

Boxes are priced separately

Cabinet boxes, face frames and end panels stay in the kitchen and get finished in place under containment. Including the interiors and shelving adds real surface area and real hours.

Condition changes everything

A ten-year-old kitchen in good shape is mostly a clean-sand-prime-spray job. A 1990s kitchen with heavy cooking grease, worn edges and a previous DIY paint job that is chipping needs significant corrective work before any finish goes on.

What different Denver kitchens actually cost

These are composite scenarios built from published ranges, not quotes. Use them to sanity-check numbers you receive.

Small galley kitchen, 14 to 18 pieces

Townhome or condo kitchen, cabinets in sound condition, doors and boxes painted one color, standard hardware reused. Expect a project toward the lower half of the published range. Site time is often three days.

Standard suburban kitchen, 28 to 36 pieces

The most common project we see across Littleton, Centennial, Aurora and Parker. Doors, drawer fronts, boxes and a pantry, moving from honey oak or dated maple to a white, greige or deep green. Expect the middle of the range, with four to five days on site.

Large kitchen with island and built-ins, 45+ pieces

Larger homes in Highlands Ranch, Castle Rock and Parker often include an island in a contrasting color, a built-in desk area and a butler's pantry. More pieces, two colors, and sometimes glass-front doors that require extra masking. Expect the upper half of the range.

Previously painted cabinets that are failing

This is the wildcard. If an earlier coating is chipping, it has to be sanded back or removed before anything new goes on. That correction can add meaningfully to the price, which is exactly why doing it right the first time is cheaper than doing it twice. How to decide in five minutes

  • Open a door and look at the box. Solid, square and clean? Refinishing is on the table.
  • Press the bottom of the sink cabinet. Soft or swollen means water damage, which may mean replacement for that run.
  • Ask whether the layout annoys you. Paint does not move a wall or add a drawer stack.
  • Count the doors. More pieces mean refinishing costs more, but replacement costs scale faster.
  • Consider your timeline. Refinishing is measured in days. Replacement is measured in weeks, sometimes months.

What a proper cabinet refinish includes

If a quote does not describe these steps, you are not comparing the same project.

  1. Degreasing. Every surface, especially around the stove and handles. Invisible kitchen grease is the number one cause of cabinet finish failure.
  2. Removal and labeling. Doors, drawer fronts and hardware removed and labeled so every piece returns to its original opening.
  3. Scuff sanding. Factory finishes are hard and slick. Sanding creates the mechanical tooth primer needs.
  4. Repairs and filling. Chips, dings and old hardware holes filled and sanded flush.
  5. Bonding primer. Formulated to adhere to cured factory finishes, thermofoil or laminate.
  6. Sprayed finish coats. Multiple thin coats of a cabinet-grade product with proper flash and cure time between them.
  7. Controlled drying. Doors finished and cured in a controlled area rather than leaned against a garage wall collecting dust.
  8. Containment in the kitchen. Boxes finished in place with plastic containment and filtration so the rest of the house stays clean.
  9. Reinstall and adjust. Hardware reinstalled, doors rehung, hinges adjusted so everything lines up.
  10. Cure guidance. Clear instructions on when to use the kitchen normally, because most cabinet finishes reach full hardness days to weeks after they are dry to the touch.

What about doing it yourself?

DIY cabinet painting is genuinely popular, and sometimes it works. Materials for a full kitchen typically run several hundred dollars once you include degreaser, sandpaper, filler, a quality bonding primer, a cabinet-grade finish and either good brushes or sprayer rental.

The honest trade-offs:

  • Time. Expect multiple weekends. Each coat needs cure time and there are two sides to every door.
  • Finish quality. Brush and roller leave texture. Sprayed finishes are why professional cabinets look like cabinets rather than like painted cabinets.
  • Space. You need somewhere clean, dust-free and temperature-stable to lay out and dry 30 doors.
  • Durability. The failure mode of DIY cabinets is almost always chipping at the edges within a year, which traces back to degreasing, sanding and primer choice.
  • Resale. A visibly amateur cabinet finish can cost more in buyer perception than the project saved.

If you do it yourself, the non-negotiables are: degrease twice, scuff sand everything, use a true bonding primer, use a cabinet-grade finish rather than wall paint, and let it cure before heavy use.

Cabinet colors Denver homeowners are choosing

Color affects cost in one practical way: coverage. Deep wood tones moving to a bright white often need an extra coat or a tinted primer, and very saturated colors can require more material.

  • Warm whites remain the most requested, because they brighten north-facing Colorado kitchens without going sterile.
  • Greige and soft gray work well with the stone and tile already common in Front Range homes.
  • Deep greens and navy on islands and lower cabinets, usually paired with a lighter upper.
  • Black and near-black for a high-contrast island, which photographs beautifully but shows dust and fingerprints.
  • Two-tone schemes continue to dominate, with lighter uppers and a stronger color below.

Red flags in a cheap cabinet quote

  • No mention of degreasing. The single most common cause of failure, skipped to save an hour.
  • "We'll scuff it" with no primer named. Bonding primer is not optional over factory finishes.
  • Wall paint specified. Latex wall paint on cabinets will mark, chip and never fully harden.
  • Doors painted on site, hung wet. Dust, sags and fingerprints follow.
  • No containment plan. Spraying boxes without containment puts overspray through your home.
  • No cure guidance. A contractor who does not tell you to go easy for a week either does not know or does not care.
  • No warranty. Cabinet finishes either hold up or they do not, and that shows within the first year.

Is cabinet painting worth it?

From a pure dollars standpoint, cabinet refinishing changes the single most visually dominant element in the room for a fraction of replacement cost. Kitchens sell houses, and cabinets are most of what people see in a kitchen photo.

From a practical standpoint, it is also the least disruptive major update available. There is no demolition, no plumbing, no countertop replacement and no weeks without a usable kitchen.

The cases where replacement genuinely wins: the layout does not work, the boxes are particleboard that has swollen from water, or you want a fundamentally different cabinet configuration. We will say so if we see it. Selling you paint over a failing box helps nobody.

What you actually get at each price point

Three quotes at $2,800, $4,900 and $7,400 for the same kitchen are usually describing three different projects. Here is what tends to separate them.

The lower tier

Typically doors and drawer fronts only, boxes left as they are or lightly coated, hardware reused, one color, and a schedule measured in two or three days. This can be a perfectly good value when the kitchen is small, the existing finish is sound and you are not changing color dramatically.

The middle tier

Doors, drawer fronts, face frames and visible box exteriors, bonding primer, two to three sprayed finish coats, filled hardware holes if you are changing pulls, and proper containment while the boxes are sprayed in place. This is where most full-kitchen projects in Littleton, Centennial, Aurora and Parker land.

The upper tier

Everything above plus interiors and shelving, a two-tone scheme, glass-front door masking, island in a contrasting color, new hardware installed with new drilling, and sometimes door modifications. Large kitchens in Highlands Ranch, Castle Rock and Parker often sit here simply because of piece count.

Why cabinet coatings cost more than wall paint

A gallon of cabinet-grade finish can cost two to three times what a gallon of standard wall paint costs, and the reason is in the chemistry rather than the marketing.

  • Hardness. Cabinet enamels are formulated to cure to a hard film that resists fingernails, pot handles and cleaning products. Wall paint never reaches that hardness.
  • Block resistance. Painted surfaces that touch each other, like a door against a frame, can stick together. Quality cabinet products are engineered to resist this.
  • Leveling. Good cabinet coatings flow out after spraying so the surface reads smooth rather than textured.
  • Adhesion systems. Bonding primers are specialty products that grip cured factory finishes, laminate and thermofoil.
  • Cleanability. Kitchens get wiped constantly. The finish has to survive degreasers, not just water.

When a quote is dramatically lower than the others, the first question to ask is which products are being used. The second is how many coats. More on how we choose products is on our paint brands page.

The preparation that decides whether it lasts

Degreasing is not optional

Cooking vapor coats everything in a kitchen, including cabinet faces nowhere near the stove. It is invisible, it does not wipe off with water, and paint will not bond through it. Professional cabinet work starts with a dedicated degreaser and ends up changing rags far more often than anyone expects.

Scuff sanding creates mechanical tooth

Factory finishes are cured hard and slick by design. Sanding is not about removing the finish; it is about giving the primer a surface to key into. Skipping it is the second most common cause of chipping.

Filling and detail work

Old hardware holes, chips at the edges, dents from years of use and worn corners all get filled and sanded flush. On a two-tone project this is also when any door modifications happen.

Primer selection

Bonding primer for most situations, with stain-blocking versions where tannin or water staining is present on wood cabinetry. Oak grain, in particular, will telegraph through a smooth finish unless it is addressed, and homeowners who want a modern slab look on oak need to understand that grain filling is a separate labor item.

Spray environment

Doors finished in a controlled area produce a noticeably better surface than doors sprayed in a dusty garage. Temperature, airflow and dust control all show up in the final result.

Hardware, hinges and the small upgrades worth doing

While cabinets are apart, several upgrades cost far less than they would as a standalone project.

  • New pulls and knobs. The cheapest way to make a refinished kitchen read as new. If hole spacing changes, old holes get filled and new ones drilled during prep.
  • Soft-close hinges. Often a straightforward swap on compatible doors, and it changes how the kitchen feels daily.
  • Drawer slide repair. Sticky drawers are easier to address with fronts removed.
  • Toe kick and filler strips. Finishing these to match completes the look, and they are easy to miss in a cheap quote.
  • Crown or trim addition. Adding simple trim to upper cabinets before painting can modernize a dated kitchen for very little material cost.
  • Interior organizers. Easier to install while doors are off.

Caring for painted cabinets so they last

A sprayed cabinet finish is durable, but the first few weeks matter and basic habits extend its life for years.

  • Go easy during cure. Most cabinet finishes are dry to the touch in hours but continue hardening for days to weeks. Avoid stacking heavy items against doors and do not scrub during that window.
  • Clean with mild soap and water. Avoid abrasive pads and harsh degreasers on the new finish.
  • Wipe spills near the sink and dishwasher. Standing water at the base of a door edge is the one thing that will eventually work on any finish.
  • Use bumpers. Small felt pads at door corners prevent contact wear.
  • Keep touch-up paint. Ask for a labeled container at the end of the project and store it somewhere you will actually find it.

What cabinet painting does for resale

Kitchens drive showings. Buyers scroll listing photos on a phone, and the kitchen photo is usually the one that decides whether they book a visit. Dated cabinets read as "project" even when the rest of the home is updated.

Three practical points for sellers:

  • Neutral wins. Warm whites, greiges and soft grays appeal to the widest buyer pool. Save the bold island color for a home you are staying in.
  • Consistency matters. Painting cabinets but leaving dated trim and wall color undercuts the effect. Our interior painting page covers packaging it together.
  • Timing. Finish cabinet work before photography, and leave cure time before move-out traffic starts.

Eight questions to ask every cabinet quote

  1. How many pieces are you counting? If the number does not match your count, the quote is for a different project.
  2. Are boxes and face frames included? This is the most common silent difference between quotes.
  3. What degreaser and what sanding step? If the answer is vague, expect chipping.
  4. Which bonding primer? By name. Wall primer is not an answer.
  5. Which finish product, and how many coats? Cabinet-grade, sprayed, with cure time between coats.
  6. Where are doors finished? A controlled area beats a dusty garage every time.
  7. How is the kitchen contained? Overspray and dust should never travel through the house.
  8. What is the warranty, and what is the cure guidance? A contractor who skips the cure conversation has not thought about year two.

Special cases that change the number

Oak and open-grain wood

Oak grain telegraphs through paint. If you want a smooth, modern finish on oak, the grain must be filled, which is a separate and significant labor step. If you are comfortable with visible grain, the project is simpler and less expensive. This is worth deciding before you receive quotes, because contractors assume differently.

Thermofoil and laminate

Both can be painted with the right bonding primer, but thermofoil that is peeling from the substrate is a repair problem first. Delaminating doors usually need replacement, after which the replacements and the existing boxes are finished together.

Glass-front and mullion doors

Masking glass takes time, and mullion grids are slow detail work. A kitchen with several glass fronts prices higher than the same piece count in solid doors.

Islands in a second color

A contrasting island means a second product setup, additional masking and separate cure cycles. It looks fantastic and it is not free.

Previously painted cabinets

If an earlier coating is failing, it has to come off or be sanded back to a sound, bondable surface. This is the single biggest wildcard in cabinet pricing, which is why we look before we commit to a number on previously painted kitchens.

Running the numbers on your own kitchen

A simple way to decide, using the published ranges in this guide:

  1. Count your pieces. Walk the kitchen and count every door and drawer front, including pantry, island and any built-ins you want matched.
  2. Decide on boxes. Doors and fronts only, visible box exteriors included, or everything including interiors.
  3. Be honest about condition. Heavy grease, worn edges or a previous paint job that is chipping all add preparation.
  4. Place yourself in the range. Small and simple sits low in the $2,000 to $8,000 published band; large, two-tone and previously painted sits high.
  5. Compare against replacement. Denver replacement quotes commonly start around $15,000 and climb past $30,000, before counters and backsplash work that often rides along.
  6. Apply the discount. Projects booked by October 31, 2026 get 25% off labor, with paint and materials excluded.

If the numbers are close, which happens in small kitchens with failing boxes, replacement may genuinely be the better investment. We will tell you when we see it rather than selling you paint over a cabinet that is coming apart.

What tends to surprise people

  • Piece count beats room size. A compact kitchen with lots of small drawers can cost more than a large kitchen with few, large doors.
  • Interiors are a real decision. Many homeowners skip them with no regret, and it meaningfully changes the number.
  • Color change can add a coat. Dark wood to bright white is the usual culprit.
  • Cure time is not padding. Rushing doors back into service is how a beautiful finish gets marked in week one.

The bottom line

Expect a professional Denver cabinet refinish to land between roughly $2,000 and $8,000 depending on the number of doors and drawer fronts, the condition of the existing finish, whether boxes and interiors are included, and the severity of the color change. Compare quotes on degreasing, sanding, primer, finish product and cure guidance rather than on the total alone.

Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to paint kitchen cabinets in Denver?

Published Denver cost data puts professional cabinet refinishing at roughly $2,000 to $8,000 for a typical kitchen. The number of doors and drawer fronts, the condition of the existing finish and whether boxes are included drive the price.

Is painting cabinets cheaper than replacing them?

Substantially. Denver replacement quotes commonly start around $15,000 and go past $30,000, while refinishing typically runs a fraction of that when the boxes are solid.

What about refacing?

Refacing, which replaces doors and drawer fronts and veneers the boxes, commonly runs about $7,000 to $15,000 in the Denver area. It makes sense when you want a different door style but like the layout.

How long does cabinet painting take?

Most Denver kitchens take three to five days on site, driven by cure time between coats. Larger kitchens with islands and built-ins take longer.

Will painted cabinets chip?

Not when the process is right. Chipping traces back to skipped degreasing, no scuff sanding, missing bonding primer, wall paint used instead of a cabinet-grade finish, or doors returned to service before the finish cured.

Can you paint laminate or thermofoil cabinets?

Often, yes, with the correct bonding primer and preparation. Thermofoil that is peeling from the substrate is a different problem and needs assessment first.

Do you paint the inside of the cabinets?

Only if you want it. Interiors and shelving add real surface area and cost, and many homeowners skip them. We price it both ways so you can choose.

Does the 25% October discount apply to cabinets?

Yes. The discount applies to the labor portion of cabinet projects booked by October 31, 2026. Paint and materials are not included in the discount.
