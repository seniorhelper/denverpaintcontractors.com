Fixing a Bad Paint Job: What It Takes and What It Costs

Peeling after one winter, roller marks, overspray on the patio, a crew that stopped answering. Here is how to assess the damage, press your case, and get it corrected properly.

By Denver Paint ContractorsUpdated October 7, 202618 minute read

Key takeaways

  • Document everything first: dated photos, the written contract, the estimate, texts and payment records.
  • Most failed work traces back to skipped preparation, not to the paint itself.
  • Repair cost is usually higher than the original job, because the failing material has to be removed before anything new goes on.
  • Colorado's sun and freeze-thaw cycles expose shortcuts within one to three seasons, which is why problems surface so fast here.
  • A written scope and a real warranty are the two things that prevent a repeat.

Start here: is it a defect or just a disappointment?

Before you call anyone, separate the two categories. They lead to different conversations.

Workmanship defects

  • Paint peeling, flaking or blistering within the first couple of years
  • Visible roller marks, lap marks, runs, sags or drips in the finish
  • Bare spots, thin coverage showing the old color through
  • Overspray on windows, roofing, concrete, landscaping or vehicles
  • Paint on hardware, hinges, glass, carpet or countertops
  • Crooked cut lines at ceilings, trim and corners
  • Failed caulk joints that were supposed to be replaced
  • Surfaces in the written scope that were never painted

Expectation gaps

  • A color that looks different on the wall than on the chip, which is normal and especially pronounced in Colorado's bright light
  • Minor texture differences where drywall was patched
  • Sheen differences between touch-up areas and the full wall
  • Normal wear after several years of service

Expectation gaps are worth a conversation. Defects are worth a documented claim.

Document it properly, today

Whether you pursue the original contractor or hire someone to correct the work, documentation decides how the conversation goes.

  1. Photograph everything in daylight: wide shots that show the elevation or room, then close-ups of each defect, then a photo of the back of a paint chip if anything is peeling.
  2. Date the photos. Phones do this automatically, and the metadata matters later.
  3. Pull your paperwork: the estimate, the contract, the scope of work, any change orders, the invoice and proof of payment.
  4. Collect communications. Texts and emails about what was promised, especially around preparation, number of coats and product.
  5. Note dates. When the work was done, when you first noticed the problem, when you reported it.
  6. Write a factual summary. One page, no adjectives: what was contracted, what was delivered, what has failed, and what you want done.

If you hire a second company to assess the work, that written assessment becomes useful evidence too. We provide a written assessment as part of our free estimate on repair projects.

Why most paint jobs fail: the short list

We get called to correct failed work constantly, and the causes repeat.

Preparation was skipped

Washing, scraping, sanding, filling, caulking and priming are the hours that cheap bids remove. You cannot see them on the estimate, and you cannot see them on day one. You see them in year two.

Painted over chalk or dirt

Weathered exterior paint develops a powdery chalk layer. Painting over it is like taping to dust. Washing is not optional on any Colorado exterior repaint.

Wrong product for the surface

Interior paint used outdoors, wall paint used on cabinets or trim, film-forming stain on a horizontal deck, standard primer over glossy enamel. Each one is a predictable failure.

Painted in the wrong conditions

Too cold, too hot, in direct sun on a baking wall, over a damp surface, or right before a storm. Our Colorado timing guide covers what the right window looks like.

Moisture source never addressed

Sprinklers hitting siding, overflowing gutters, a bathroom fan vented into the attic, a roof leak. Paint over an active water source always loses.

Thin coverage

Stretching material across more square footage than it is rated for produces a film too thin to protect anything, which fails early even when everything else was done correctly.

Your options with the original contractor

1. Call them first, in writing

Even if you are furious, the cleanest path starts with a written request. Email or text beats a phone call because it creates a record. State what failed, attach photos, reference the contract language, and ask for a specific remedy with a date.

2. Check the warranty terms

Read exactly what was promised. Many painting warranties cover workmanship for a stated period. Ours is five years in writing, which is the standard worth asking for. If the contractor gave only a verbal promise, you have less to work with, which is itself a lesson for next time.

3. Escalate with documentation

If the contractor stops responding, your paperwork and photos become the case. Many homeowners find that a clear, unemotional written summary, delivered once, gets more movement than repeated angry calls.

4. Know the limits

Some contractors disappear, dissolve the business, or simply refuse. At that point the practical question becomes what it costs to correct the work and who is doing it, not how to win an argument.

We are not attorneys and this is not legal advice. For anything involving significant money, a consumer protection attorney or a mediation service is worth the call.

What repairs actually cost

Here is the part homeowners find hardest to hear: fixing a failed paint job usually costs more than the original job should have cost.

Why repairs cost more

  • Removal is extra work. Nobody had to scrape the failing paint the first time. Now someone does.
  • Damage compounds. Water that got behind a failing film may have swollen trim, lifted siding or damaged drywall.
  • Blending is hard. Repairing a section means matching sheen and color against surfaces that have already aged.
  • The cause still has to be fixed. Sprinklers, gutters, ventilation or caulk that caused the failure get addressed before any painting.

What can be repaired and what has to be redone

Usually repairable

  • Isolated peeling with a single identifiable cause, like one sprinkler head
  • Drips, sags and runs, which sand out and recoat cleanly
  • Overspray on glass and hard surfaces
  • Poor cut lines at ceilings and trim
  • Missed surfaces and thin spots on an otherwise sound finish

Usually a full redo

  • Widespread peeling across multiple elevations
  • Paint applied over chalk across an entire house
  • Wrong product for the substrate, such as interior paint used outdoors
  • Cabinets painted without degreasing or bonding primer, chipping everywhere
  • Film-forming stain peeling across a deck surface

The test we use is simple: can the remaining coating be made into a sound, bondable surface without more labor than starting over? If yes, repair. If no, redo.

How we correct another company's work

  1. Free assessment, in writing. We photograph and document what failed and name the probable cause. You can use that document however you like, including with the original contractor.
  2. Find the cause. Moisture sources, substrate condition, chalk testing, adhesion checks in several areas.
  3. Define the real scope. What has to come off, what can stay, what trades are needed first. No optimistic guessing.
  4. Remove failing material. Scraping, sanding, stripping or media-appropriate removal depending on the surface and the home's age.
  5. Repair the substrate. Wood filler, drywall repair, carpentry referral for rot, masonry attention where needed.
  6. Correct the cause. Caulk joints, sprinkler advice, gutter and drainage notes, ventilation recommendations.
  7. Prime for the situation. Bonding, stain-blocking, tannin-blocking, masonry or rust-inhibiting as the surface requires.
  8. Repaint with named products. Sherwin-Williams, PPG or Behr lines specified in writing for each surface.
  9. Warranty it. Five years on our workmanship, in writing.

Why Colorado exposes bad work faster

Homeowners in milder climates sometimes get away with a weak paint job for years. Here, the clock runs faster.

  • UV at altitude. The World Health Organization notes UV increases roughly 10 percent per 1,000 meters of elevation, and Denver sits near 1,600 meters. Thin or low-grade films chalk and fade quickly.
  • Freeze-thaw. National Weather Service records show Denver freezes occurring as early as September 8 and as late as June 8. Every cycle works on any water that got behind the film.
  • Daily temperature swings. Substrates expand and contract constantly, stressing coatings and caulk that were not chosen for movement.
  • Hail. Storms open brittle coatings mechanically, giving water a path.

The practical implication: a Colorado paint job that was going to fail usually announces itself within one to three seasons. That is bad news if you hired wrong, and useful news if you are still deciding who to hire.

How to never do this twice

Everything below is free to ask for and takes minutes.

  • Get the scope in writing, surface by surface, including what is excluded.
  • Require named products. Brand, line and sheen for each surface. "Premium paint" means nothing.
  • Require coat counts. Plus primer where the surface needs it.
  • Ask for the preparation list. Washing, scraping, sanding, filling, caulking, priming, itemized.
  • See the insurance certificate. Colorado has no statewide painting license, so insurance and documentation carry more weight here.
  • Get the warranty in writing, with its length and what it covers.
  • Be suspicious of very low bids. On exteriors, preparation is the largest block of labor, and it is the easiest thing to quietly delete.
  • Avoid large cash deposits with no paperwork.
  • Check that the company still exists in a year. Reviews, a real phone number and a physical business presence all matter.

What to say when you call for a second opinion

You do not need to know the technical vocabulary. Useful information sounds like this:

  • When the work was done and by whom, if you are comfortable saying
  • What was supposed to be included
  • When you first noticed the problem
  • Where it is worst, and which direction that wall faces
  • Whether there is any water source nearby: sprinklers, gutters, a bathroom, a roof issue
  • Whether anything has been touched up already

Send that with photos and you will get a far more useful answer than a generic price range.

Six failure patterns we see across the Denver metro

After enough repair calls, the same stories repeat. If one of these sounds like your house, you are not an unlucky outlier.

The two-summer exterior

A homeowner takes the lowest of three bids on a full exterior. It looks great in September. The following spring there is hairline cracking at the trim joints, and by the second spring the south elevation is peeling in palm-sized flakes. The cause is almost always the same: the house was never washed, chalk was never removed, bare wood was never spot-primed, and the film was spread thin to make the material budget work.

The disappearing cabinet painter

Cabinets look spectacular for about three months. Then the edges near the handles start chipping, the area around the stove gets gummy, and a door shows a fingernail-width line of old finish at the bevel. Nobody degreased, nobody sanded, and the primer was a general-purpose wall primer rather than a bonding product.

The painted-over moisture problem

A bathroom ceiling or a basement wall gets repainted twice in one year by two different crews. Both times it comes back. Nobody checked the exhaust fan, the roof flashing or the grading, so the actual water source is still arriving on schedule.

The sprinkler stripe

A beautiful exterior fails in a horizontal band along the bottom two rows of siding, usually on one or two elevations. Irrigation has been soaking the same boards every morning since May. The paint was fine. The water was not.

The rushed fall exterior

Work gets squeezed into late October because the crew is behind schedule. Daytime temperatures look fine on the forecast, but overnight lows drop below the product's minimum and the film never cures properly. By spring it is chalky, soft and peeling at the edges.

The handyman special

Interior walls, trim and doors all painted with the same low-cost flat wall paint. The walls look acceptable. The trim and doors mark with a fingernail, never harden, and stick to each other when closed, because wall paint was never engineered for that service.

What a repair estimate should spell out

Repair quotes are easier to pad and easier to under-scope than new-work quotes, because nobody knows exactly how much failing material will come off until the scraping starts. A credible repair estimate handles that honestly.

  • A stated cause. Not "paint failure", but the actual mechanism: moisture at the sills, chalk under the film, no bonding primer on the cabinets.
  • Removal scope. How far back the failing material will be removed, and what happens if more comes off than expected.
  • Substrate repair. Wood filler, drywall patching, sealing end grain, or a referral out for carpentry or roofing.
  • Cause correction. Caulk joints, sprinkler adjustment notes, ventilation recommendations, downspout extensions.
  • Primer specified by problem. Bonding, stain-blocking, tannin-blocking, masonry or rust-inhibiting.
  • Finish product and sheen per surface, by brand and line.
  • Blending plan. Whether the repair stops at a natural break line or the whole elevation or room gets coated for uniform appearance.
  • Warranty. Whether the repair carries the same warranty as new work. Ours does.

If a bid says "scrape and repaint as needed" and nothing else, it is not a plan. It is an invitation to argue later.

Why "just touch it up" usually does not work

Homeowners naturally want the smallest possible repair. Sometimes that works. Often it does not, and here is why.

Color has moved

Colorado sun fades exterior color measurably within a couple of seasons. A perfect color match to the original formula will not match the wall next to it. We often recommend repainting to a natural break, such as a corner board, a trim line or an entire elevation, so the eye never sees a seam.

Sheen telegraphs

Even when color matches, a touch-up patch reflects light differently than aged surrounding paint. On flat interior walls you can sometimes get away with it. On satin and semi-gloss, almost never.

Texture changes

Scraped, sanded and filled areas have a different surface profile. Without feathering and sometimes a skim coat, the repair shows in raking light regardless of color.

The failure may not be finished failing

Paint adjacent to a peeling area is often already compromised. Repairing a two-foot square and leaving marginal paint around it buys a season, not a fix. A good crew tests adhesion outside the obvious failure zone before deciding where to stop.

Hail, insurance and paint damage

Front Range hail complicates the question of who pays. A few practical points:

  • Paint damage alone is usually not a claim. Insurers typically respond to structural damage such as dented siding, damaged roofing or broken windows.
  • Paint repairs can ride along with a covered claim. If siding or trim is being replaced, the painting of those replaced elements often belongs in the scope.
  • Document immediately. Date-stamped photos of chips, dents and damaged trim right after a storm are far more persuasive than photos taken in spring.
  • Do not let a storm chaser scope your paint. Out-of-town crews that appear after a hail event are a known problem in Colorado. Hire the same way you would any other year: written scope, insurance certificate, local presence, real warranty.
  • Prime exposed material quickly. A chip in the film is an open door. Even if the full repaint waits for the claim, spot-priming protects the substrate through the winter.

Nine questions for whoever fixes it

  1. What do you believe caused this, and what are you basing that on?
  2. How will you confirm the cause before painting?
  3. How far back will you remove failing material, and what if it keeps coming?
  4. Which primer are you using, and why that one for this problem?
  5. What product line and sheen are going on top?
  6. Where will you stop, and how will you keep the repair from showing?
  7. What other trades might be needed, and will you tell me before spending my money on paint?
  8. What warranty applies to the repair?
  9. Can I see your insurance certificate?

Nine questions, five minutes, and a much better chance of never reading an article like this again.

Building a record that actually helps you

Whether you end up negotiating, mediating or simply hiring someone else, a clean record saves time and money. What a useful file looks like:

  • The signed contract or estimate, with the scope language highlighted.
  • Every written communication, exported rather than screenshotted where possible, in date order.
  • Payment records, showing amounts and dates.
  • Dated photos at three distances: whole elevation or room, the defect area, and a close-up with something for scale.
  • A timeline document listing what was promised, when work occurred, when problems appeared and when you reported them.
  • A third-party written assessment describing the probable cause in technical terms.
  • Weather notes if conditions during application are relevant, since public climate records are available for the dates in question.

Keep it factual. The strongest version of this file contains no adjectives at all.

The five contract lines that prevent most disputes

  1. Surfaces included and excluded, written as a list rather than a sentence.
  2. Preparation steps by surface, naming washing, scraping, sanding, filling, caulking and priming where each applies.
  3. Products by name, including manufacturer, line, sheen and number of coats.
  4. Change-order process, stating that additional work requires written approval of cost before it proceeds.
  5. Warranty terms, including length, what is covered, what is excluded and how to request service.

Those five lines take a contractor ten extra minutes to write and eliminate most of what people argue about later. Our estimates include all five by default, and our warranty page spells out the coverage in plain language.

A word about the part nobody writes about

Discovering that a major home investment failed is genuinely stressful. People describe feeling embarrassed, as though they should have known better. Almost nobody should have known better, because the things that were skipped were deliberately invisible.

What helps: get the facts documented, get one credible assessment of the cause, then make a decision based on what the house needs rather than on how the last experience went. The second project tends to go well precisely because the homeowner now asks the right questions.

Choosing the next contractor after a bad experience

People who have been burned once often swing to one of two extremes: hiring the cheapest option again because "they all cut corners anyway," or assuming the most expensive bid must be the safe one. Neither is a strategy.

What actually predicts a good outcome is documentation. A contractor who writes down surfaces, preparation, products, coats, schedule and warranty is telling you how they operate before a single ladder goes up. A contractor who resists writing it down is telling you the same thing in a different way.

Four signals that a company is built to finish well

  • They diagnose before they price. On a repair, that means checking adhesion and looking for moisture rather than quoting from the driveway.
  • They say no to some work. A company that tells you the siding needs carpentry before paint, or that the layout problem will not be solved by color, is protecting your money rather than closing a sale.
  • They explain the sequence. If someone can walk you through why primer A goes on surface B, they have done this a lot.
  • They are reachable. One number, answered, by people who were there.

What we do differently on repair work

If they are not, it tells you exactly what the corrective project involves so you can compare bids on the same basis. Either way you end up better informed than you were, which after a failed paint job is worth something on its own.

When a second opinion is worth paying for

Most reputable painting companies, including ours, will assess failed work for free as part of an estimate. There are a few situations where a paid, independent opinion is worth it anyway: when the dollar amount in dispute is large, when the original contractor is still engaged and disputing the cause, or when an insurance claim is involved and the cause of damage is contested. An independent inspector who does not want the repair work has no stake in the answer, and that neutrality can carry more weight in a disagreement than a competing contractor's assessment. For everyday peeling and chipping, a free written assessment from an experienced painting company is usually all anyone needs.

The bottom line

A failed paint job is not usually a paint problem. It is a preparation problem, a product-selection problem or an unaddressed moisture problem, and it shows up fast in Colorado. Document what happened, understand the cause, and insist that whoever corrects it addresses the cause rather than covering it.

Quick answers

Frequently asked questions

What should I do if a painter did a bad job?

Document everything with dated photos, gather your contract, estimate and payment records, and make a written request to the contractor describing the defects and the remedy you want. If they do not respond, get a written assessment from another company so you know the real cause and cost.

Who pays to fix a bad paint job?

If the work is within a written warranty period and the failure is a workmanship defect, the original contractor should. If they refuse or are gone, the practical path is correcting the work with a reputable company and preserving your documentation.

Does repainting cost more after a failed job?

Usually yes, because the failing material has to be removed and any damage underneath corrected before new coatings go on. Removal is work nobody paid for the first time.

How soon does a bad paint job show up in Colorado?

Often within one to three seasons. High-altitude UV, freeze-thaw cycles and large daily temperature swings expose thin films and skipped preparation faster than milder climates do.

Can peeling paint be repaired without repainting everything?

Sometimes. Isolated failures with a single clear cause can be repaired and blended. Widespread peeling, paint over chalk, or the wrong product for the substrate generally require a full redo.

What if the painter is no longer in business?

Your options narrow to correcting the work and preventing a repeat. Keep your documentation, get a written assessment of the cause, and choose the next contractor on written scope, named products, insurance and a real warranty.

How do I avoid hiring the wrong painter next time?

Require a written scope with preparation steps, named product lines and sheens, coat counts, proof of insurance and a written warranty. Then compare bids on those lines rather than on the total.

Do you fix other companies' painting work?

Yes, regularly. We assess the cause in writing, remove what is failing, correct the underlying problem and repaint with named products, backed by our 5-year workmanship warranty.
