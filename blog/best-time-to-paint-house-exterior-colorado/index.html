The best time to paint a house exterior in Colorado: a month-by-month guide for Denver homeowners

Freeze dates, label temperatures, afternoon storms and booking lead times, laid out month by month so you can pick the right window and actually get on the calendar.

By Bighorn PaintingUpdated September 16, 202615 minute read

Key takeaways

  • Late spring through early fall gives Denver the most dependable exterior painting weather.
  • The National Weather Service lists Denver's average last spring freeze around May 5 and average first fall freeze around October 7.
  • Follow the minimum temperature on the paint label, and remember the overnight low matters as much as the afternoon high.
  • Colorado's dry air, strong sun and wind can make paint dry too fast, so crews plan each wall around the sun.
  • Book early. Good summer dates fill up, and HOA color approval can add weeks before a start date.

The quick answer

For most of the Denver metro, the most reliable window for exterior painting runs from mid-to-late spring through early fall. Late May, June, September and early October are often the sweet spots: warm enough for paint to cure, without the hottest, stormiest stretches of midsummer. July and August work too, with smart scheduling around heat and afternoon thunderstorms.

But "best time" isn't only about weather. It's also about when you can get a good crew on your calendar and whether your HOA needs to approve colors first. The homeowners who get the dates they want are usually the ones who start planning in late winter.

Why timing matters so much for exterior paint

Paint isn't dry when it feels dry. It goes through a curing process where the film forms, bonds to the surface and hardens. If the temperature drops too low, the surface gets wet, or the paint dries too fast, that process can go wrong. The results show up later as poor adhesion, cracking, uneven sheen, lap marks or early peeling.

Every exterior paint has application guidelines on the label and the manufacturer's data sheet. They typically cover minimum air and surface temperature, how long to keep the surface dry after application, and recoat times. Some modern exterior products are formulated to be applied at lower temperatures than older paints. PPG, for example, says its Permanizer exterior line can be applied at temperatures as low as 35°F. But every product is different, and a good crew follows the label for the specific product on your house, not a general rule of thumb.

Colorado adds some twists that make those rules harder to follow than they look on paper.

Denver's painting climate, by the numbers

The National Weather Service office in Boulder tracks Denver's freeze and snow history going back to the 1800s. A few numbers from those records shape exterior painting season:

Denver climate markerWhat the NWS records showWhat it means for paint
Average last spring freezeAround May 5Earlier spring work is possible, but cold nights are still a real risk
Latest spring freeze on recordJune 8, 2007A late cold snap can happen even after a warm May
Average first fall freezeAround October 7Fall work usually wraps up by mid-October
Earliest first freeze on recordSeptember 8 (1962 and 2020)Early September cold snaps happen, so watch forecasts
Average first measurable snowAround October 18Late October exteriors are a gamble

Averages are just that. Any given year can run early or late, which is why crews watch the multi-day forecast, not just today's high.

Why the overnight low matters

A sunny 65°F afternoon in April can be followed by a night in the 20s. Paint applied in the afternoon may still be curing when the temperature crashes, and dew or frost can settle on the surface. That's why many painters treat the overnight low as the real limit, and why early spring and late fall exterior days need careful judgment.

Strong sun and fast drying

Denver's elevation means more intense sunlight. The World Health Organization notes UV increases about 10 percent for every 1,000 meters of altitude. Combine that with low humidity and wind, and paint on a hot, sun-baked wall can skin over too quickly. That leads to lap marks and brush marks and can affect how well the paint levels and bonds. The fix isn't to avoid summer. It's to follow the shade around the house.

Month-by-month exterior painting calendar for Denver

Here's how the year typically plays out along the Front Range. Elevation matters: foothills communities run cooler and have shorter windows than the plains east of Denver.

January and February

Exterior: Generally not recommended. Cold temperatures, frozen surfaces and snow make proper application and curing unreliable. Best use of the month: Get exterior estimates, pick colors, submit HOA approvals and reserve a spring or early summer start date. This is also prime time for interior painting, which runs year-round inside heated homes.

March

Exterior: Occasional warm stretches happen, but March is historically one of Denver's snowier months, and nights stay cold. Small touch-ups on a warm, dry, sunny wall might be possible, but full exterior repaints are risky. Best use: Walk around your house after the winter and note peeling, open caulk joints and damaged trim. Photograph it for estimates.

April

Exterior: Some good days, especially late in the month, but frequent temperature swings and spring snowstorms keep April unpredictable. Prep work like washing can start on the right days. Best use: Finalize contracts and colors. HOAs often see a rush of spring submissions, so get yours in.

May

Exterior: The season opens up. Early May can still bring a freeze, so crews watch overnight lows closely, but mid-to-late May is often excellent: mild temperatures, strong daylight, and walls that aren't yet baking. Watch for: Spring storms and the start of hail season.

June

Exterior: One of the best months of the year for exterior painting in Denver. Warm, long days and generally reliable overnight temperatures. Watch for: Afternoon thunderstorms that can pop up quickly, and hail. Crews plan to have fresh paint protected before storms roll in.

July

Exterior: Very workable with good scheduling. The hottest days make sun-facing walls too hot to paint well in the afternoon, so crews start on the west side in the morning and move to the east side later. Watch for: Monsoon-pattern afternoon storms and fast-drying conditions.

August

Exterior: Similar to July. Heat and afternoon storms require planning, but there are plenty of good painting hours. Best use: If you haven't booked a fall exterior yet, do it now. September calendars fill up.

September

Exterior: Often ideal. Warm days, cooler nights and fewer storms. Early September freezes are uncommon but not unheard of, as the NWS records show, so crews still watch the forecast. Best use: A great month to repaint before winter so failing paint doesn't sit through another freeze-thaw season.

October

Exterior: The first half of the month can be very good, especially with sunny, mild spells. By mid-to-late October, the average first freeze and first snow dates make exterior work increasingly risky. Best use: Wrap up exteriors early in the month, and stain decks while conditions allow.

November and December

Exterior: Generally not recommended aside from very small jobs on unusually warm, dry days. Best use: Interior projects. Holiday prep painting, basements, bedrooms, trim and cabinets all fit well here.

The best time of day to paint an exterior

Choosing the right month is half the battle. The other half is choosing the right hours on each wall.

Follow the shade

A wall in direct afternoon sun can be far hotter than the air temperature. Paint applied there can dry too fast. Experienced crews work the east side in the morning after dew burns off, then move around the house so each wall is painted out of the harshest direct sun.

Wait for dew to dry

On cool mornings, surfaces can hold dew even when the air feels dry. Painting over a damp surface hurts adhesion. A common painter guideline is to keep the surface temperature comfortably above the dew point, and to always follow the product's surface-temperature instructions.

Stop early on cold evenings

In spring and fall, crews finish earlier in the day so fresh paint has time to set before temperatures drop and evening moisture arrives.

Respect the storm forecast

Summer afternoon thunderstorms can hit fast. Fresh paint needs time before it's rain-ready, and each product lists its own guidance. Crews plan work so newly painted areas aren't exposed when a storm is likely.

Colorado weather risks and how painters work around them

Hail

The Front Range sees hail mostly in late spring and summer. Hail can chip and dent trim, siding and garage doors, and can crack older, brittle paint. If a storm damages your home, photograph it and check with your insurer about coverage before repairs.

Wind

Wind accelerates drying, blows dust onto wet paint and makes spraying impractical on some days. Brush-and-roll methods and wind-aware scheduling help.

Wildfire smoke

Some summers bring smoky days. Ash and dust settling on fresh paint can affect the finish, so crews may adjust plans on heavy-smoke days.

Temperature whiplash

Big daily swings stress paint and caulk as materials expand and contract. That's one reason flexible, exterior-rated caulks and properly bonded coatings matter so much here. Our guide to why exterior paint fails in Colorado goes deeper.

How elevation changes your painting window

The Denver metro isn't one climate zone. A home on the plains in Aurora or Thornton may have a longer, warmer season than a home in the foothills near Evergreen or Morrison. Higher-elevation and west-side neighborhoods tend to see later spring freezes and earlier fall cold, and shaded mountain lots stay cold longer. If you live at higher elevation, plan for a tighter window and book earlier.

Local notes for a few metro communities:

  • Highlands Ranch sits higher than central Denver and requires HRCA color review, so build approval time into the schedule.
  • Aurora and Thornton neighborhoods on open ground get intense sun and wind, which affects daily timing.
  • Littleton and Centennial homes with mature trees can have shaded walls that dry slowly after cool nights.

The best time to stain a deck in Colorado

Deck staining follows similar weather logic, with a few differences:

  • The wood must be dry. After rain or deck washing, wood needs time to dry before stain can soak in. The stain maker's instructions say how long.
  • Avoid the hottest part of the day. Stain on hot boards can dry before it penetrates, leaving blotches.
  • New pressure-treated lumber may need to weather first. Many stain makers recommend waiting before staining new treated wood, so check the product guidance.
  • Spring and early fall are often ideal. Moderate temperatures give stain time to absorb.

More on the process is in our deck staining section.

What to paint in the off-season

Cold months are perfect for work inside. Winter interior projects to consider:

  • Bedrooms, living rooms and hallways that have gotten scuffed
  • Ceilings with old stains, primed with a stain blocker
  • Kitchen cabinets, which transform a home without a remodel
  • Trim, baseboards and doors that have yellowed or chipped
  • Basements and bonus rooms

Keep ventilation in mind when windows stay closed, and ask about low-VOC or zero-VOC options if anyone in the home is sensitive to odors.

What happens when paint goes on in the wrong conditions

Most weather-related paint problems don't show up the same day. They show up weeks or months later, which is why a painter racing the calendar can leave a job that looks fine at the walkthrough and fails after the first winter.

Too cold

If temperatures fall below what the product is designed for, the paint film may not form properly. That can lead to poor adhesion, cracking and a finish that wears out early. Cold, damp mornings can also leave a thin layer of moisture on the surface that interferes with bonding.

Too hot or in direct sun

On a sun-baked wall, paint can dry before it levels out, leaving lap marks, brush marks and uneven sheen. Heat can also contribute to blistering when moisture or solvents get trapped under a film that skinned over too fast.

Rain or storms too soon

Rain on fresh paint can cause streaking, surfactant leaching that leaves a sticky or discolored residue, and in worse cases washed-out areas that need to be redone. Checking the forecast and the product's dry-to-rain guidance prevents most of it.

Wind and dust

Windy days blow grit, pollen and dust onto wet paint, leaving a rough texture, and make spray application hard to control. Crews may switch methods or shift which walls they work on.

Sequencing painting with other exterior projects

If you're planning several exterior updates, the order matters. Paint generally comes after the work that could damage it or change the surfaces being painted.

  • Roof replacement: Roofing work can scuff siding and trim and drop debris. Many homeowners schedule roofing first, then paint.
  • Window or door replacement: New windows and doors change trim and caulk lines. Install first, then paint and caulk around them.
  • Siding repair or replacement: Replace damaged boards before painting so everything gets primed and coated together.
  • Gutters: Fixing overflowing gutters first prevents repeat peeling on fascia and siding.
  • Landscaping and irrigation: Adjust sprinklers so they don't hit freshly painted siding, and trim plants back from walls before painters arrive.
  • Deck rebuilds: Build or repair first, then stain once the wood is ready according to the stain maker's guidance.

After hail storms, many Colorado homeowners handle insurance-related roof and siding repairs first. Coordinating the timing so painting follows those repairs avoids painting twice.

Questions to ask your painter about weather and scheduling

  • What are the temperature limits for the specific product you're using on my house?
  • How do you decide when it's too cold, too hot or too windy to paint?
  • What happens to my schedule if a storm or cold snap rolls in?
  • Which sides of the house will you paint at which times of day?
  • How long does the siding need to dry after washing before you paint?
  • If weather affects the finish, how is that handled under your warranty?

A contractor who answers these clearly is one who plans around Colorado weather instead of hoping it cooperates. Our guide to choosing a painter covers more questions worth asking.

Doing interior and exterior in the same year

Planning both? A simple strategy works well in Denver: book interior work for late winter or early spring, and exterior work for late spring, summer or early fall. You get the interior done while exterior calendars are still waiting on weather, you finalize exterior colors with the interior palette in mind, and you avoid having crews in your home during your busiest summer months. Bundling can also simplify scheduling and may reduce setup costs. Our ways to save page explains why.

A seasonal exterior checklist for Denver homeowners

Painting season goes smoother when the house is ready for it. Use this checklist twice a year.

Late winter to early spring

  • Walk the house after snowmelt and photograph peeling, cracking and open caulk joints.
  • Check fascia and soffits for signs of ice dams or gutter overflow.
  • Look at the bottom rows of siding where snow piled up against the walls.
  • Get estimates and choose colors while crews still have open calendars.
  • Submit HOA color approval if your community requires it.

Late summer to early fall

  • Look for hail damage from spring and summer storms on trim, siding and garage doors.
  • Check south and west walls for new fading and chalking.
  • Recaulk small gaps before freeze-thaw season starts.
  • Stain decks and fences while temperatures are moderate.
  • Shut off or adjust sprinklers that spray siding before winterizing irrigation.

Why late-season painting bargains can backfire

As the weather cools, some homeowners are offered a quick exterior at a tempting price just to squeeze in one more job before winter. Sometimes a warm October stretch really is fine, and a careful crew will use it well. The risk comes when the schedule, not the weather, drives the decision.

Paint applied on a day that's warm enough at noon can face a hard freeze that night, before it has properly set. Shorter days mean less drying time before evening moisture settles on the surface. And if the job gets interrupted by an early snow, you can end up with a half-finished house through winter. If a late-season date is offered, ask how the crew will handle overnight lows, what product is being used and its temperature limits, and what happens if weather forces a stop. If the answers are vague, waiting for spring is usually the better deal.

The bottom line

In Denver, the best time to paint your house exterior is generally from mid-to-late spring through early fall, with June and September often standing out. Within that window, the details make the difference: following label temperatures and overnight lows, working around sun, wind and storms, and giving HOAs time to approve colors. Start planning in late winter, and you'll get the dates you want with a crew that isn't rushing to beat the weather.

Frequently asked questions

What month is best to paint a house exterior in Denver?

June and September are often the most dependable months for exterior painting in Denver, with warm days and more reliable overnight temperatures. Late May, July, August and early October can also work well with careful scheduling.

How cold is too cold to paint outside in Colorado?

It depends on the product. Every exterior paint lists a minimum application temperature on its label, and some modern exterior paints can be applied at lower temperatures than older formulas. The overnight low matters too, so painters watch the forecast, not just the afternoon high.

When is the last freeze in Denver?

The National Weather Service lists Denver's average last spring freeze around May 5, though freezes have occurred as late as June 8. The average first fall freeze is around October 7.

Can you paint a house exterior in the summer heat?

Yes, with smart scheduling. Crews avoid painting walls in the hottest direct sun, working each side of the house when it is in shade, and plan around afternoon thunderstorms.

How far ahead should I book an exterior painter in Denver?

Many homeowners start getting estimates in late winter for late spring or summer work. Booking early helps you get preferred dates and leaves time for HOA color approval.

Can you paint a house exterior in winter in Colorado?

Full exterior repaints are generally not recommended in winter because cold temperatures, frozen surfaces and snow interfere with application and curing. Winter is a good time for interior painting instead.

How long does exterior paint need before rain?

Each product lists its own guidance for how long fresh paint should stay dry. Crews check the forecast and plan work so newly painted areas are not exposed to likely storms.

When should I stain my deck in Colorado?

Spring and early fall are often ideal for deck staining because moderate temperatures let stain soak in. The wood must be dry, and staining in the hottest part of a summer day can cause uneven results.
